Three useful distinctions

UMN vs LMN

Established UMN syndromes often include spasticity, brisk reflexes, and an extensor plantar response. LMN lesions cause weakness with reduced tone/reflexes; denervation may cause atrophy and fasciculations. Acute central lesions may initially be flaccid.

Sensory ataxia vs cerebellar ataxia

Loss of proprioceptive input causes visual dependence and worsening balance when the eyes close. Cerebellar ataxia remains evident with the eyes open. A positive Romberg sign is not specific to dorsal column disease.

At the level vs below the level

Cord lesions may produce segmental LMN or sensory findings at the lesion and long-tract findings below it. Pain–temperature loss from a unilateral cord lesion typically begins about 1–2 segments below the lesion, with variation.

Scope, anatomical qualifications, and sources

This teaching schematic covers the major voluntary motor routes and somatic sensory routes commonly used in clinical localization. It does not include every descending tract, autonomic pathway, special sensory system, or reflex circuit. It illustrates one representative input from each side rather than somatotopy or individual dermatomes.

Routes are schematic and not drawn to anatomical scale. Diagonal crossing segments form an X exactly at the anatomical midline. Rounded bends are used elsewhere. The outline is a frontal schematic of anatomical levels, not a literal anatomical view or cross section. Path crossings without a neuron circle do not indicate synapses. Dots indicate signal direction only; their speed is an animation setting, not axonal conduction velocity. A lesion stops the illustrative signal, but interrupted dots do not imply that all downstream neurons become electrically silent.

The corticospinal view shows the dominant crossed lateral tract. Anterior corticospinal, bilateral axial, and indirect motor projections are omitted. The corticobulbar view is a facial example, not a universal crossing rule for every cranial motor nucleus. Upper facial input is bilateral and lower facial input predominantly contralateral; forehead sparing is a useful classical tendency, not an absolute rule. The pontine crossing is a conceptual simplification: facial corticobulbar fibers have variable lower-brainstem courses.

The facial touch view emphasizes the crossed trigeminothalamic projection. Bilateral and uncrossed projections, particularly for oral input, are omitted. Facial proprioception and the mesencephalic trigeminal system are not shown. The dorsal spinocerebellar view includes a parallel upper-limb cuneocerebellar route described in its steps. The ventral spinocerebellar view uses the classical predominantly double-crossed route; not every fiber follows that pattern.

Clinical presets represent selected structures, not exact vascular territories or a diagnostic calculator. The cord examples use a generic thoracic level unless a cervical lesion is specified. Spinal shock can obscure early UMN signs. Bladder and bowel involvement depends on lesion extent and timing.
